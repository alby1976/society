# Governance Principles

## 1. Purpose

This document states the governance principles of the Christian Community Transformation Society.

These principles guide the interpretation of the [[International Constitution]], [[International Governance Framework]], board policies, national chapter documents, ministry frameworks, reporting systems, and institutional memory practices.

## 2. Christ's Lordship

The Society belongs to Christ.

Governance is not a merely technical activity. It is stewardship before God. The Society's boards, officers, advisors, members, workers, and volunteers must understand authority as service under Christ.

Governance must therefore be humble, accountable, prayerful, truthful, and mission-oriented.

## 3. Scripture as Supreme Authority

Scripture is the Society's supreme written authority.

Every governance document is subordinate to Scripture. Where a policy is shown to contradict Scripture, the policy must be corrected.

This principle protects the Society from legalism, pragmatism, trend-chasing, and institutional self-preservation.

## 4. Confessional Clarity

The Society is confessionally Reformed.

Confessional clarity helps preserve doctrine across countries and generations. The Society should not treat theological precision as an obstacle to charity. Sound doctrine protects charity from becoming sentimental, secular, or confused.

## 5. Church-Supporting Boundaries

The Society supports faithful local churches and does not replace them.

This principle applies to:

- chaplaincy;
- discipleship;
- prayer gatherings;
- housing communities;
- worker cooperatives;
- leadership development;
- grants;
- international expansion.

The Society must never become a substitute church with a nonprofit costume.

## 6. Mission Before Institution

The institution exists to serve the mission.

The Society must resist decisions that preserve the organization while weakening its purpose. Growth, funding, property, influence, and reputation are not ultimate goods.

A smaller faithful Society is better than a larger confused one.

## 7. International Unity with Local Wisdom

The Society preserves one doctrine, one mission, and one constitutional identity across national chapters.

At the same time, local chapters need wisdom to obey local law, serve local communities, work with local churches, and communicate in local languages.

The principle is:

> one identity, many lawful local expressions.

## 8. Reserved Powers and Delegated Authority

Good governance distinguishes what must remain international from what may be delegated locally.

Reserved powers protect the Society's identity.

Delegated authority allows ministry to function.

Both are necessary. Reserved powers without delegation create bottlenecks. Delegation without reserved powers creates drift.

## 9. Accountability

Authority must be accountable.

Accountability includes:

- board oversight;
- reporting;
- financial controls;
- theological review;
- mission alignment review;
- conflict of interest disclosure;
- document review;
- member and stakeholder communication;
- lawful compliance.

Accountability is not distrust. It is how trust is protected.

## 10. Transparency with Proper Confidentiality

The Society should be transparent in governance while protecting confidential information.

Transparency does not mean exposing pastoral, employment, legal, personal, or sensitive financial information. Proper confidentiality is not secrecy; it is stewardship.

## 11. Prayerful Decision-Making

Prayer should accompany major decisions, especially:

- founding steps;
- constitutional adoption;
- board appointments;
- national chapter formation;
- major partnerships;
- housing acquisition;
- cooperative launch;
- foundation grants;
- chaplaincy appointments;
- mission alignment reviews;
- crisis response.

Prayer is not a decorative paragraph at the end of a policy. It is dependence on God.

## 12. Written Governance

Important governance should be written down.

The Society should avoid relying on memory, personality, or informal understandings when written clarity is needed.

Written governance includes:

- policies;
- charters;
- frameworks;
- procedures;
- minutes;
- decisions;
- reports;
- registries;
- review schedules.

If a future leader cannot find it, it might as well have been written on a napkin in invisible ink.

## 13. Institutional Memory

The Society should preserve its history, not merely its current policies.

Institutional memory includes:

- why decisions were made;
- alternatives considered;
- founding principles;
- lessons learned;
- crises endured;
- ministries launched;
- ministries ended;
- theological concerns addressed;
- national chapters recognized;
- major prayers and providences remembered.

Institutional memory helps future leaders remain faithful without needing to rediscover everything.

## 14. Stewardship of Resources

The Society's resources belong to God.

Resources include:

- money;
- property;
- documents;
- reputation;
- time;
- volunteer energy;
- staff capacity;
- relationships;
- data;
- institutional knowledge.

Stewardship requires financial controls, budgeting, reporting, reserve planning, risk management, and review.

## 15. Protection of the Vulnerable

The Society's ministries may serve people facing hardship, loneliness, poverty, housing instability, employment difficulty, spiritual need, or crisis.

Governance must therefore include safeguards appropriate to the ministry.

Compassion without safeguards can harm the people it means to help.

## 16. Subsidiarity

Decisions should be made at the lowest level competent and authorized to make them, unless the matter touches reserved powers, doctrine, mission, major risk, or international identity.

This allows local action while protecting the whole Society.

## 17. Proportionality

Governance requirements should be proportionate to risk and authority.

A small local volunteer activity should not need the same process as launching a national housing trust. But every activity still needs appropriate accountability.

## 18. Review and Correction

The Society should regularly review its documents, ministries, chapters, and decisions.

Review is not failure. Review is maintenance. Even good governance gathers dust.

When correction is needed, the Society should prefer timely correction over denial, delay, or blame-shifting.

## 19. Leadership as Service

Leadership in the Society is stewardship and service.

Leaders should be selected not merely for skill, wealth, influence, charisma, or availability, but for mission alignment, character, wisdom, humility, theological seriousness, and ability to work within accountable governance.

## 20. No Founder Dependency

Founders may provide vision, history, and wisdom, but the Society must not depend permanently on any one founder.

The Society should preserve founding principles through documents, advisors, board culture, and institutional memory rather than personal control.

## 21. Lawful Compliance

The Society and its national chapters should obey applicable civil law unless obedience would require disobedience to God.

Local legal requirements should be documented and integrated into national chapter governance.

Legal compliance is not the mission, but it is part of good stewardship.

## 22. Mission Alignment

All Society activity should be evaluated against the mission.

The question is not merely, "Is this useful?"

The better questions are:

- Does this advance the Society's mission?
- Does it support faithful local churches?
- Does it preserve theological identity?
- Does it use resources wisely?
- Does it create unhealthy dependency?
- Does it require authority we do not have?
- Does it expose the Society to unwise risk?
- Does it help future leaders remain faithful?

## 23. Review

These Governance Principles should be reviewed annually and whenever the Society materially revises its constitution, governance framework, board structure, national chapter model, or mission alignment review system.
