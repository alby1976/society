# Pilot Cooperative Policy

## 1. Purpose

This policy establishes standards for launching, operating, reviewing, scaling, pausing, or closing a pilot worker cooperative.

The Society has accepted the principle that a pilot cooperative may precede a full Church Support Foundation because a pilot requires fewer resources and allows the Society to test governance, work, reporting, risk, and mission alignment before building larger structures.

---

# 2. Governing Principle

Start small enough to learn, but serious enough to be honest.

A pilot is not pretend governance. It is real work with limited scale, defined authority, clear reporting, prayer, review, and an exit plan.

---

# 3. Pilot Objectives

A pilot cooperative may test:

- worker participation;
- service model;
- training model;
- financial viability;
- Society oversight;
- reporting system;
- leadership capacity;
- church-supporting boundaries;
- chaplaincy support;
- legal structure;
- customer demand;
- pricing;
- worker development;
- mission alignment.

---

# 4. Pilot Approval

A pilot cooperative should not begin without approval.

Approval should include:

- mission case;
- activity description;
- legal review;
- budget;
- leadership;
- worker status;
- insurance;
- reporting plan;
- risk review;
- conflict of interest disclosure;
- time limit;
- evaluation criteria;
- authority limits;
- closure plan.

---

# 5. Pilot Scope

The pilot must define:

- services offered;
- target customers;
- workers involved;
- manager;
- budget limit;
- time period;
- geographic area;
- tools or equipment;
- legal form;
- reporting schedule;
- decision authority.

Scope creep should be resisted unless approved.

A pilot that quietly becomes permanent without review is just mission drift wearing a starter jacket.

---

# 6. Legal and Employment Review

Before operating, the pilot should review:

- employment status;
- contractor status;
- volunteer rules;
- cooperative law;
- tax obligations;
- insurance;
- occupational health and safety;
- privacy;
- contracts;
- liability;
- charity or nonprofit limits.

The pilot must not rely on legal assumptions.

---

# 7. Financial Plan

The pilot financial plan should include:

- startup cost;
- operating budget;
- pricing;
- expected revenue;
- payroll or compensation;
- equipment;
- insurance;
- software or tools;
- reserves;
- accounting process;
- loss limit;
- funding source.

The pilot should define how much loss the Society is willing to absorb.

---

# 8. Worker Participation

Pilot workers should understand:

- their legal status;
- compensation;
- expectations;
- training;
- supervision;
- safety;
- participation rights;
- grievance process;
- spiritual care availability;
- confidentiality;
- reporting expectations.

No worker should be told vague promises of ownership without written explanation.

---

# 9. Chaplaincy and Church Boundaries

The pilot may include chaplaincy support.

Spiritual care must be voluntary and not tied to work performance.

The pilot should encourage local church connection where appropriate but must not become a substitute church.

---

# 10. Reporting

The pilot shall report using [[Worker Cooperative Manager 5-15 Report]] or another approved reporting tool.

Reports should cover:

- work completed;
- worker development;
- revenue;
- costs;
- customer issues;
- risks;
- support needed;
- prayer requests;
- mission alignment concerns;
- decisions needed.

---

# 11. Evaluation Criteria

Before launch, the pilot must define success criteria.

Criteria may include:

- mission alignment;
- worker participation;
- worker development;
- legal compliance;
- financial performance;
- customer satisfaction;
- service quality;
- leadership capacity;
- reporting discipline;
- risk management;
- church-supporting posture.

---

# 12. Pilot Review

The pilot should be reviewed at defined intervals.

Review questions:

- Should the pilot continue?
- Should it scale?
- Should it pause?
- Should it close?
- What policy changes are needed?
- What did workers learn?
- What did leaders learn?
- What risks emerged?
- Did the pilot support mission?
- Did it create unhealthy dependency or confusion?

---

# 13. Scaling

A pilot may scale only after review.

Scaling may require:

- board approval;
- legal update;
- stronger governance;
- additional insurance;
- financial controls;
- worker agreements;
- customer contracts;
- safety procedures;
- manager training;
- mission alignment review;
- revised reporting.

Scaling should not happen merely because leaders are excited.

---

# 14. Pause or Closure

The pilot may be paused or closed if:

- legal concerns arise;
- finances are unsustainable;
- worker safety is at risk;
- mission alignment is weak;
- reporting fails;
- leadership capacity is insufficient;
- conflict becomes unmanageable;
- customer demand is too low;
- quality cannot be maintained.

Closure should protect workers and document lessons learned.

---

# 15. Institutional Memory

At the end of a pilot stage, the Society should preserve:

- launch rationale;
- decisions;
- reports;
- financial summary;
- worker feedback;
- customer feedback;
- risks;
- lessons learned;
- recommendations;
- board decision;
- next steps.

The pilot should teach future leaders even if it fails.

---

# 16. Review

This policy should be reviewed before each pilot launch and after each pilot evaluation.
