# Leadership Health and Workload Policy

## 1. Purpose

This policy establishes how the Society reviews and responds to leadership workload, spiritual health, family responsibilities, role overload, and sustainability concerns.

The Society has adopted the principle that leadership qualification includes whether a leader is spending appropriate time with God and family where applicable. If leadership responsibilities prevent this, the Society should reduce workload, provide support, restructure duties, or remove the person from leadership until conditions change.

## 2. Governing Principle

The Society should not use leaders until they are empty and then call it faithfulness.

Leadership health is a governance concern because exhausted, isolated, or spiritually neglected leaders can harm themselves, their households, the people they serve, and the mission entrusted to them.

## 3. Scope

This policy applies to board members, executive directors, national chapter leaders, ministry leaders, chaplains, housing managers, cooperative managers, Foundation directors, committee chairs, formation team leaders, and other persons with significant responsibility.

## 4. Workload Warning Signs

Warning signs may include:

- chronic missed reports;
- repeated missed meetings;
- inability to complete duties;
- irritability or harshness;
- secrecy;
- poor judgment;
- family distress connected to workload;
- neglect of prayer or spiritual responsibilities;
- refusal to rest;
- inability to delegate;
- excessive dependence on one person;
- increasing conflict;
- avoidable errors;
- persistent exhaustion.

## 5. Routine Workload Review

Leaders should be reviewed periodically.

Review may ask:

- Is the role still realistic?
- Are duties clear?
- Is the leader overloaded?
- Are reports being submitted?
- Is the leader receiving support?
- Are family responsibilities being neglected because of Society workload?
- Is time with God being crowded out by role demands?
- Is delegation needed?
- Should authority be reduced or role restructured?

## 6. First Response: Support and Reduction

When workload is unhealthy, the first response should ordinarily be supportive correction.

Possible responses:

- reduce workload;
- clarify role;
- add assistant or co-leader;
- pause non-essential tasks;
- extend deadlines;
- provide administrative support;
- reduce meeting burden;
- appoint interim help;
- require rest period;
- provide pastoral or chaplaincy encouragement where appropriate;
- adjust reporting format.

The first response should not be shame. It should be truthful care.

## 7. Second Response: Restriction or Leave

If support does not improve the situation, the Society may restrict duties or require leave.

This may include temporary reduction of authority, pause on new responsibilities, removal from a specific committee, leave from leadership duties, requirement to complete overdue reports before resuming, supervision increase, or role reassessment.

Restriction should be documented and reviewed.

## 8. Third Response: Removal or Suspension

If workload, neglect, or incapacity continues and harms the leader, household, team, or mission, removal or suspension may be appropriate.

Removal or suspension may be temporary or permanent depending on circumstances.

This should be handled with confidentiality, compassion, legal compliance, and mission protection.

## 9. Family Responsibilities

Where applicable, leaders should not be expected to sacrifice ordinary family responsibilities for Society growth.

Board and executive leaders should watch for patterns where a leader's household is consistently harmed by Society work.

If a leader cannot serve while maintaining appropriate family responsibilities, the Society should adjust the role or remove the person from that role until circumstances change.

## 10. Time with God

Where a role requires spiritual leadership or mission-bearing responsibility, the leader's own spiritual health matters.

The Society should not appoint or continue leaders who are so consumed by Society work that ordinary prayer, worship, repentance, and dependence are neglected.

This policy does not create intrusive spiritual surveillance. It creates a governance duty to address obvious and confessed patterns of unhealthy overload.

## 11. Confidentiality

Workload and spiritual health reviews may involve sensitive information.

Records should be limited, respectful, and need-to-know.

Do not turn personal struggle into public meeting material.

## 12. Reporting

Leadership health concerns should be reported at the appropriate level.

Reports should focus on role capacity, support needed, risks, decisions required, and whether workload adjustment is needed.

Private details should be minimized.

## 13. Corrective Action

Where workload creates risk, a corrective action plan may be created.

The plan should include issue, workload reduction, support, reporting changes, review date, criteria for restoration, and authority responsible.

## 14. Restoration

A leader may return to greater responsibility when workload is sustainable, reports are current, authority is clear, family and spiritual responsibilities are no longer being crowded out by role demands, risk is reduced, and supervising authority approves.

Restoration should be documented.

## 15. Review

This policy should be reviewed annually and after any significant leadership overload, suspension, resignation, or removal.
