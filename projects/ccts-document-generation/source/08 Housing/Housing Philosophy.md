# Housing Philosophy

## 1. Purpose

This document explains the theological and ministry philosophy behind the Society's housing work.

Housing ministry is not merely a practical response to market conditions. It is a form of Christian stewardship and mercy that seeks to support stable households, faithful work, church connection, community life, and care for vulnerable persons.

This document should be read with [[Housing Trust Framework]], [[Stewardship Housing Policy]], [[Mission Statement]], and [[Core Values]].

---

# 2. Governing Principle

Housing is a place where ordinary life happens before God.

People pray, eat, rest, argue, repent, work, recover, raise children, host neighbors, and face hardship in homes. Housing ministry therefore touches discipleship, dignity, mercy, stewardship, and community.

The Society's housing philosophy begins with the conviction that practical care and spiritual concern belong together.

---

# 3. Creation and Home

God created human beings for embodied life, community, work, rest, and relationship.

Housing is not ultimate, but stable housing often supports faithful ordinary life. Unstable housing can intensify poverty, isolation, family stress, employment difficulty, and spiritual discouragement.

The Society may therefore treat housing as a serious ministry concern.

---

# 4. Mercy and Responsibility

Christian mercy should be compassionate and truthful.

Housing ministry should not humiliate residents or treat them as projects. It should also avoid creating irresponsibility or dependency where wise accountability is needed.

Mercy with truth asks:

- What does this person or family need?
- What responsibilities remain theirs?
- What support is appropriate?
- What would create unhealthy dependency?
- What would promote stability and dignity?
- What would encourage connection to faithful community?

---

# 5. Stewardship

Property belongs to God and must be stewarded.

The Society must manage housing with:

- financial responsibility;
- maintenance planning;
- legal compliance;
- resident care;
- risk management;
- transparency;
- mission alignment;
- long-term affordability where possible.

Neglected property is not humility. It is deferred chaos with plumbing.

---

# 6. Hospitality

Hospitality is a Christian virtue.

Housing ministry can embody hospitality by creating spaces of welcome, safety, stability, and care.

Hospitality does not mean boundarylessness. Good hospitality includes order, expectations, and protection.

---

# 7. Community

Housing ministry should encourage healthy community life.

Community may include:

- neighborly care;
- shared responsibility;
- prayer opportunities;
- conflict resolution;
- mutual respect;
- connection to churches;
- support for families;
- care for vulnerable persons.

Community cannot be forced by policy alone, but policy can make space for it.

---

# 8. Church-Supporting Identity

Housing ministry should support local churches.

The Society may encourage residents toward faithful local churches where appropriate. It may offer chaplaincy, prayer, and spiritual care. It may partner with churches for support and referral.

But housing ministry must not become a substitute congregation.

---

# 9. Non-Coercion

The Society may maintain an explicitly Christian housing mission.

However, the Society must avoid using housing vulnerability to coerce religious participation.

Residents should understand the Society's Christian identity, community expectations, and available spiritual support before entering agreements.

Transparency is better than pressure.

---

# 10. Affordability

Affordable housing should be pursued where possible and lawful.

Affordability should be defined by policy and local context.

The Society should consider:

- income levels;
- local market rents;
- resident needs;
- operating costs;
- subsidies;
- donor restrictions;
- maintenance reserves;
- long-term sustainability.

Affordability that ignores long-term costs may collapse and harm the people it meant to help.

---

# 11. Long-Term Stewardship

The Society should prefer models that preserve mission and affordability over time.

This may include:

- community land trust structure;
- long-term leases;
- nonprofit ownership;
- charitable housing corporation;
- restrictions on resale or transfer;
- mission covenants;
- reserve funds;
- board oversight.

The structure must be legally reviewed in each jurisdiction.

---

# 12. Housing and Work

Housing may support meaningful work.

Stable housing can help workers participate in training, cooperative work, ministry service, and family life.

Worker housing must be handled carefully to avoid employer control, dependency, or conflict between employment and tenancy.

---

# 13. Housing and Chaplaincy

Chaplaincy may support housing residents through prayer, listening, encouragement, referral, and crisis care.

Chaplaincy should be voluntary, respectful, confidential, and church-supporting.

Residents should not be made to feel that access to housing depends on revealing private spiritual matters.

---

# 14. Housing and Justice

The Society should pursue housing justice in a biblical sense: fairness, care for the vulnerable, honest dealing, protection from exploitation, and stewardship before God.

The Society should not adopt ideological frameworks that contradict its theological commitments.

---

# 15. Housing and Mission Drift

Housing ministry carries mission drift risk.

Risks include:

- property concerns dominating mission;
- financial pressure weakening Christian identity;
- resident selection becoming arbitrary;
- spiritual care becoming coercive;
- church connection disappearing;
- real estate goals replacing mercy;
- debt driving decisions;
- legal complexity overwhelming governance.

Regular Mission Alignment Review is required.

---

# 16. Review

This philosophy should be reviewed annually during housing development and whenever the Society launches a housing trust, acquires property, or changes housing policy.
